On April 7, 2026, a retired history teacher from Phoenix named Robert tried to do what he had done half a dozen times before: apply for a Venezuelan tourist visa to visit Angel Falls with his wife. He booked a flight to Mexico City to drop off his passport at the Venezuelan consulate — the nearest one that still served U.S. citizens.

When he arrived, the consulate window was closed. A single sheet of paper was taped to the glass: “Todos los trámites de visa son ahora 100% en línea en cancilleriadigital.mppre.gob.ve — efectivo 6 de abril de 2026.”

Robert had just become one of the first Americans to discover, the hard way, that Venezuela’s entire visa process had changed overnight. No more passport drop-offs. No more paper forms. No more begging a consulate in Mexico City or Havana to fit you in. Everything — application, payment, approval, delivery — now happens through a single government portal. And the fee tripled.

If you’re a U.S. citizen planning to visit Venezuela in 2026, this guide will walk you through the new e-Visa system end to end: what changed on April 6, how to apply through the MPPRE portal, what the new fees and validity periods actually are, which documents will get you approved (and which mistakes will get you rejected), and how to handle the two requirements that trip up the most Americans — proof of onward travel and proof of accommodation.

Quick answer

U.S. citizens now apply for a Venezuelan tourist visa through the official MPPRE electronic portal at cancilleriadigital.mppre.gob.ve. The new e-Visa costs approximately USD 180, is valid for one year with multiple entries, allows stays of up to 90 days per entry, and is processed in roughly 15 days. You must upload a passport scan, biometric photo, proof of a return or onward flight, proof of accommodation, and a travel itinerary. The U.S. State Department currently classifies Venezuela as a Level 4 “Do Not Travel” destination — you need to understand the risks before you apply.

What Actually Changed on April 6, 2026

For most of the last decade, getting a Venezuelan visa as an American has been a special kind of bureaucratic theater. Venezuela broke diplomatic relations with the United States in early 2019, and every Venezuelan consulate on U.S. soil closed. Americans who wanted to visit were forced to apply at Venezuelan consulates in third countries — most commonly Mexico City, Havana, or Port of Spain — by mailing their physical passport abroad and waiting weeks for it to come back with a stamp, or traveling there in person.

On April 6, 2026, Venezuela’s Ministry of Popular Power for Foreign Affairs (MPPRE) launched a fully digital visa system and shut down the old paper process simultaneously. The stated goal, per the MPPRE press release, was to modernize consular services and reduce processing times. The practical effect for U.S. citizens was three things:

  • No more mailing your passport. Your passport stays with you. The visa is delivered electronically as a PDF you print and carry.
  • No more consulate visit. You register, pay, upload documents, and receive approval entirely online.
  • The fee jumped. The old paper tourist visa for U.S. citizens was roughly USD 60. The new e-Visa is reported at USD 180 — a 3× increase that reflects a new consular fee structure introduced with the digital rollout.

Processing time, on the other hand, has dropped sharply. Under the old system, Americans routinely waited 4–8 weeks. Under the new e-Visa, Venezuela is targeting approximately 15 business days, with some applicants reporting approvals in as little as 3–5 days during the early rollout.

Do U.S. Citizens Actually Need a Venezuelan Visa?

Yes — without exception. The United States is not on Venezuela’s visa-exemption list. Regardless of the length of your stay, your reason for traveling, or whether you’re flying in from Bogotá or Miami, you must hold a valid Venezuelan visa before you board your flight.

This is one of the key differences between Venezuela and most of the rest of Latin America. Americans can enter Colombia, Peru, Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Ecuador, and the entire Central American bloc visa-free. Venezuela stands alone in the region as a country where an American passport gives you zero automatic entry rights. If you’re unsure whether a visa is required for your specific passport and purpose, run a quick check on the Venezuela visa checker for U.S. citizens before you do anything else.

Who Uses the Venezuela e-Visa Today

In practice, the U.S. citizens currently applying for Venezuelan e-Visas fall into five buckets:

  • Venezuelan-Americans visiting family. The largest group — dual nationals and naturalized Americans with relatives still in Caracas, Maracaibo, or Valencia.
  • Journalists, academics, and researchers. Professionals applying for journalist, student, or business visas through the same MPPRE portal.
  • NGO and humanitarian workers. Staff of organizations operating in Venezuela under specific programs.
  • Adventure tourists. A small but steady stream of travelers visiting Canaima National Park, Angel Falls, Los Roques, and the Gran Sabana.
  • Business travelers. Oil, mining, and commodities professionals on short assignments.

The 5 Required Documents (And Where Americans Go Wrong)

Every e-Visa application requires exactly five uploaded files. The portal is strict about file size, format, and readability. Blurry scans, wrong dimensions, and expired documents are the three most common reasons applications stall or get rejected outright.

1. Passport Bio-Data Page Scan

A color scan or high-resolution photo of the page with your photo, full name, date of birth, passport number, and expiration date. Your passport must be valid for at least 6 months beyond your planned departure date from Venezuela. PDF or JPG, typically under 2 MB.

2. Digital Passport Photo

A recent (less than 6 months old) color photograph against a plain white background, no glasses, no hats, no smiling. Most Americans upload a photo taken at a CVS or Walgreens passport photo station and it works perfectly.

3. Proof of Onward or Return Travel

This is the single most misunderstood requirement in the Venezuelan e-Visa process. You must show a confirmed flight reservation out of Venezuela within the validity period of your visa. MPPRE will not approve an application that shows a one-way ticket in.

The trap most first-time applicants fall into is buying a fully-paid return ticket before the visa is approved. Venezuelan visa approval is not guaranteed. If your application is rejected or delayed, you’re holding a $900–$1,400 ticket you can’t use. This is exactly the scenario embassies and consular officers worldwide advise travelers to avoid, and it’s why the concept of a flight reservation (held, verifiable, but not paid-for) exists. 4. Proof of Accommodation

You need to show where you will sleep every night you’re in Venezuela. MPPRE accepts three types of accommodation proof:

  • A hotel reservation covering the full duration of your stay (or the stay declared on the application).
  • A formal invitation letter from a host in Venezuela, signed, notarized if possible, with a copy of the host’s cédula (Venezuelan national ID) attached.
  • A company letter if you’re traveling for business, confirming where the company is housing you.

The most practical option for almost all tourists is a hotel reservation without prepayment. Just as with flights, you don’t want to prepay for weeks of hotel stays in Caracas or Puerto Ordaz before your visa is approved — if you’re denied, that money is gone. A free-cancellation booking held at a real hotel with a valid confirmation number is what MPPRE wants to see.

5. Travel Itinerary and Proof of Funds

A simple itinerary showing where you’ll go and what you’ll do, plus evidence that you can financially support yourself. MPPRE typically wants to see the equivalent of USD 50 per day of stay available in a bank account. The standard way to prove this is a recent bank statement (last 3 months) showing the balance.

For the best results, match the dates in your itinerary exactly to your flight reservation and hotel booking. Inconsistent dates across documents are the #1 reason visa officers worldwide flag applications for additional review — and Venezuelan consular officers are no exception.

Step-by-Step: How to Apply on the MPPRE Portal

The full MPPRE e-Visa application takes most applicants 30–45 minutes, assuming all your documents are ready beforehand. Here’s exactly what happens.

Step 1: Create an Account at cancilleriadigital.mppre.gob.ve

Navigate to the official portal and create a personal account using your full name (exactly as it appears on your passport), email, and a phone number. You’ll receive a verification email. Click the link to activate your account.

Step 2: Select Visa Type

Choose your visa category. For most travelers this will be Visa de Turismo (tourist), but business and short-visit categories are also available. The form is in Spanish by default; use your browser’s translation feature if needed, but double-check the translated field labels against the original Spanish so you don’t mis-enter data.

Step 3: Fill in Personal and Travel Details

The application form asks for passport details, personal information, emergency contact, planned date of entry, planned date of exit, cities you’ll visit, and the purpose of travel. Be specific and consistent with the documents you’re about to upload.

Step 4: Upload the 5 Documents

Upload your passport scan, biometric photo, flight reservation, hotel booking, and itinerary/bank statement in the exact order the form requests. Most file uploads have a 2 MB size limit, so compress your PDFs if needed.

Step 5: Pay the Consular Fee Online

The portal accepts international credit cards and, in some cases, wire transfers in USD. The fee is typically USD 180 for U.S. citizens applying for tourist or business visas. Payment is non-refundable, so only submit when you’re confident the rest of your file is complete and accurate.

Step 6: Wait for the Email

You’ll receive a tracking number immediately. The approval email typically arrives in 3–15 days. When approved, your e-Visa is attached as a PDF. Print two copies — one to carry in your passport, one as a backup in your carry-on.

The State Department Travel Warning: What It Actually Means

As of 2026, the U.S. Department of State classifies Venezuela as a Level 4: Do Not Travel destination. This is the highest warning level the State Department issues and applies to fewer than 20 countries worldwide. The specific risks cited include wrongful detention, kidnapping, violent crime, civil unrest, poor health infrastructure, and the absence of a U.S. embassy inside the country to provide consular support.

What this practically means for you as an American traveler:

  • No U.S. embassy in Caracas. If something goes wrong — lost passport, medical emergency, arrest — the U.S. government has very limited ability to help you on the ground. The closest full-service U.S. consular support is in Bogotá, Colombia.
  • Travel insurance is essential. Most standard policies exclude countries under Level 4 advisories, so you’ll need specialty coverage from a provider like Global Rescue or World Nomads Explorer Plus.
  • Enroll in STEP. The U.S. State Department’s Smart Traveler Enrollment Program lets you register your trip so the nearest embassy (Bogotá) knows you’re in Venezuela.
  • Your Venezuelan visa does not override the advisory. MPPRE approving your e-Visa has no bearing on U.S. government recommendations. These are two unrelated systems.

None of this is legal advice or a recommendation. Many Americans still travel to Venezuela safely each year — typically visiting family, working with NGOs, or on closely-managed tour operator itineraries to places like Canaima and Los Roques. But you should go in with your eyes open and understand that your safety net is significantly thinner than in any other country in the Americas.

Arrival at Caracas: What Happens at Simón Bolívar International Airport

Most international flights to Venezuela land at Simón Bolívar International Airport (CCS) in Maiquetía, about 21 km from central Caracas. Having a valid e-Visa PDF is necessary but not sufficient. Venezuelan immigration (SAIME) still asks incoming travelers to present the same documents you uploaded to get the visa: your printed e-Visa, your return flight, your accommodation proof, and proof of funds.

Bring physical printed copies of everything. Immigration officers at CCS frequently request paper documents even when you can pull them up on your phone. The wait times at immigration vary wildly — 15 minutes on a good day, two hours when two international flights arrive at the same time. Allow plenty of buffer for any connecting transport.

Other Entry Airports

Although CCS handles the vast majority of international traffic, a handful of other Venezuelan airports see direct international flights in 2026:

  • Valencia — Arturo Michelena International (VLN): limited regional flights.
  • Maracaibo — La Chinita International (MAR): primarily regional and cargo.
  • Porlamar — Del Caribe Santíago Mariño International (PMV): seasonal charter and leisure flights to Margarita Island.

If you’re flying into any of these, the same document rules apply, and the same immigration procedure runs. If you need to contact a Venezuelan diplomatic mission at any point in the process, the Venezuelan embassy finder lists current locations and contact details.

Extending Your Stay: The SAIME Process

The 90-day tourist stay can be extended once for an additional 90 days at any office of SAIME (Servicio Administrativo de Identificación, Migración y Extranjería), Venezuela’s equivalent of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services. You must apply before your original 90 days expire.

The extension is in-person only. Bring your passport, your printed e-Visa, your entry stamp, proof of accommodation for the extension period, and be prepared to pay a local fee in bolívares or U.S. dollars. Extensions are discretionary, not automatic — SAIME staff may ask detailed questions about why you need more time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does the Venezuela e-Visa cost for U.S. citizens in 2026?

Approximately USD 180 for a tourist or business visa, paid online during the application through the MPPRE portal. This is up from roughly USD 60 under the old paper system and is non-refundable regardless of outcome.

Can I apply without a return or onward flight ticket?

No. The MPPRE e-Visa application will not accept a submission without proof of how you plan to leave Venezuela within the stay you’ve declared. The simplest way to satisfy this requirement without paying for a real ticket you may not be able to use is a verifiable Venezuela flight reservation (MyJet24 Premium, $7.90) held for the duration of the application process.

How long does the Venezuela e-Visa take to process?

Official guidance is approximately 15 business days, but many early applicants have reported approvals in 3–15 days. Processing during the first weeks of the new system was noticeably faster than MPPRE’s official estimate.

Do I still need to go to a Venezuelan consulate?

No. The entire process — application, document upload, payment, and visa delivery — runs through the MPPRE portal. Your passport stays with you at all times. This is the biggest single change from the pre-April 2026 system, which required mailing or hand-delivering your passport to consulates in third countries like Mexico.

How long is the Venezuela tourist visa valid?

The e-Visa is valid for 12 months from the date of issue, with multiple entries allowed during that period. Each individual entry permits a stay of up to 90 days, extendable once for 90 more days in-country at a SAIME office.

Is travel insurance required?

Venezuela does not currently require travel insurance as part of the e-Visa application, but it is strongly recommended. Because Venezuela is a U.S. State Department Level 4 destination, standard travel insurance often excludes coverage — you’ll need a specialty policy that specifically covers Level 4 advisory countries.

What happens if my visa is denied?

The USD 180 fee is non-refundable. You’ll receive a rejection email through the portal, and you may reapply once you’ve addressed the reason for denial. This is the single strongest argument against paying for real tickets and hotel rooms upfront: if you’re denied, everything that isn’t refundable becomes a sunk cost. You can read about the full playbook in our guide to what happens when your visa is refused and how to recover.

Can I enter Venezuela by land instead of flying?

Yes, in principle, but the U.S. State Department strongly advises against all land border crossings, especially between Venezuela and Colombia, due to the risk of detention and violent crime. The safest entry point for American citizens is Simón Bolívar International Airport (CCS). Your e-Visa is valid at any approved port of entry.

Key Takeaways

  • Venezuela launched a fully online e-Visa system on April 6, 2026. The old paper consulate process is gone.
  • U.S. citizens need a visa without exception. There is no visa-on-arrival and no visa-free window.
  • Expect to pay ~USD 180. Valid 12 months, multiple entry, up to 90 days per stay (extendable).
  • Five documents are required: passport scan, biometric photo, onward flight, accommodation proof, itinerary with proof of funds.
  • Use a dummy ticket and a dummy hotel booking to avoid losing money on real tickets and hotel stays before approval.
  • Processing takes ~15 business days (sometimes much faster during the early rollout).
  • Venezuela is a U.S. State Department Level 4 country. Plan accordingly — specialty insurance, STEP enrollment, and realistic expectations about available U.S. support on the ground.
